# Why 1 in 3 People on Earth Drive on the Wrong Side

**Target runtime:** ~3 min (~450 words)
**Thumbnail text option:** "The 'wrong' side isn't wrong"

## Cold Open (~20s / ~50 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [MAP: world map, countries that drive on the left highlighted in red — about a third of the globe] | "About a third of the world drives on the left. Not a random third, either — it's almost every former British colony, plus a handful of holdouts. And the reason traces back to something that has nothing to do with cars at all: swords." |

## Context & Stakes (~25s / ~60 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [B-ROLL: illustration or stock image of a medieval road with horseback riders] | "Long before engines, most people were right-handed, and most people carried a weapon. Riding on the left meant your sword arm faced oncoming traffic — ready, just in case. It wasn't law. It was just safer, so it became habit." |

## Beat 1: Napoleon flips the switch (~30s / ~75 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [GRAPHIC: simple timeline, 1789 French Revolution → Napoleonic era] [ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Right-hand traffic laws, 1794"] | "That changed with the French Revolution. Riding on the right became a way to signal solidarity with commoners, not aristocrats. Napoleon then exported right-hand traffic across the empire he conquered — Germany, Poland, much of Western Europe all switched because France told them to." |

## Beat 2: Britain never got the memo (~25s / ~60 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [MAP: zoom to British Isles, then pan out to former British Empire territories] | "Britain, notably, was never conquered by Napoleon — so it kept driving on the left. And because Britain then built the largest empire in history, it exported that habit to India, Australia, South Africa, Japan, and dozens of other territories." |

## Beat 3: Cars just inherited the rule (~25s / ~55 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [B-ROLL: early 1900s automobile footage] | "By the time cars showed up, the side of the road was already decided. Car makers just built around it — steering wheels on the right in left-driving countries, and vice versa. Nobody chose this for cars. They inherited it from horses, and horses inherited it from swords." |

## Complication (~30s / ~65 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [GRAPHIC: bar chart or map callouts showing Sweden's 1967 switch, and a few countries that changed sides] | "The system isn't fixed, though. Sweden drove on the left until 1967, then switched almost overnight to match its neighbors — a single day, remembered as Dagen H. A few former colonies have also flipped sides since independence, usually to align with trading partners instead of old empires." |

## Resolution / Takeaway (~20s / ~50 words)
| VISUALS | NARRATION |
|---|---|
| [MAP: return to opening world map, red highlight remains] | "So the next time you're a passenger in a country where the driver's seat is on the 'wrong' side, remember: it's not wrong. It's just a rule about swords that outlived the swords, the horses, and the empires that carried it — and somehow still decides which side of the road a third of the planet drives on." |
