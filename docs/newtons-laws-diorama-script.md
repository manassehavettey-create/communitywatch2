# Paper-Cutout Diorama Animation Script
## Topic: Newton's Law of Motion

---

## 1. CONCEPT BLOCK

**Working title:** *The Orchard and the Orbit*

**Style:** A tilt-shift documentary-diorama photography look — miniature-set aesthetic, shallow depth of field, muted naturalistic archival color grading, cinematic letterboxed 9:16 framing with elegant serif documentary caption text — fused with vintage editorial paper-collage art: visible fine paper grain and fiber texture, cleanly cut character edges with soft-shadow fraying, intentionally raw torn deckle-paper edges on background elements, faint halftone newsprint dot-texture, thin strips of aged red paper tape as scene/transition accents, and subtle grid or graph-paper texture on card backings, all built from layered textured cardstock and corrugated cardboard, lit with warm tungsten key light and cool ambient fill, naturalistic soft-edged shadows, fine film grain, and floating dust motes — handcrafted but photographically real, never flat, glossy, or digital.

**Format:** 9:16 vertical, thin cinematic letterbox bars top and bottom

**Runtime:** 82 sec (15 story shots + 1 bonus CTA shot)

**Main character:** Isaac Newton, grounded in the real historical record — specifically the Isaac Newton of 1665–1687, the plague years at Woolsthorpe Manor through the writing of the *Principia Mathematica*. Physical description for consistency across every shot: a gaunt scholarly man in his mid-forties, built from layered parchment-toned cardstock standing in for skin, with visible paper-fiber texture; a long paper wig made of fine hand-cut silver-grey ringlet strands framing a narrow face with deep-set fine ink-line eyes and a furrowed, contemplative brow; wearing a burgundy velvet-textured cardstock coat with wide turned-back cuffs over a white linen-textured paper cravat and waistcoat; a small hand-cut brass-toned paper prism pinned like a badge at his lapel as his one distinguishing detail; long-fingered, slightly oversized cut-paper hands built for expressive gesture; an expression that is quietly intent and searching, as if always mid-thought.

## 5. AUDIO NOTES

**Music arc across the 16 shots:** Open with a sparse, intimate solo strings-and-piano theme under Shots 1–3, evoking a quiet countryside documentary. As Newton begins forming his ideas (Shots 4–7), introduce a single recurring pizzicato motif that ticks like a subtle metronome, mirroring the mechanical order he is discovering. Through the rising complication and Second Law (Shots 8–9), add light percussion and a modest string swell to build stakes without becoming grand. At the cannon thought experiment and Third Law climax (Shots 10–11), the arrangement should peak with fuller orchestration and a percussive accent timed to the collision impact. Recede into a warm, hushed string bed for the synthesis and fallout (Shots 12–13), letting the music breathe with the candlelight pacing. Rebuild gently but with more scale and wonder through the legacy and outro (Shots 14–15), resolving on a warm, unresolved-feeling final chord that suggests ongoing motion rather than a hard ending. Shot 16 drops the score down to a light, friendly sting with a soft paper-rustle accent, ending on a clean button.

**Recurring SFX:** Paper rustle and crinkle under every character movement and camera move; soft footsteps on dirt/paper-grass for walking shots; a faint quill-scratch sound during writing/sketching shots; a subtle wind ambience in orchard exteriors; a low candle-flicker crackle in interior night shots; a light mechanical creak for the pulley rig; a soft paper-thud and confetti-scatter sound at the apple's fall and the sphere collision; a distant birdsong/countryside ambience under the title and outro cards; a gentle paper-tape peel sound as a transition accent between select shots.

**Narrator tone:** Warm, measured, documentary narrator — unhurried pacing, a slightly hushed and reverent register during the intimate discovery beats, allowing more clarity and lift at the climax and legacy shots, and settling back into warmth for the closing line. Think public-television natural-history narration: curious, precise, never rushed, treating Newton's ideas as something to be gently uncovered rather than announced.
