# Paper-Cutout Diorama Script — "How the First Watch Was Made"

## 1. CONCEPT BLOCK

- **Working title:** The Nuremberg Egg — How the First Watch Was Made
- **Style:** A hybrid of tilt-shift documentary-diorama photography (miniature-set aesthetic, shallow depth of field, muted naturalistic/archival color grading, cinematic letterboxed 9:16 framing with elegant serif documentary captions) and vintage editorial paper-collage art (visible fine paper grain and fiber texture, cleanly cut character edges with soft-shadow fraying, intentionally raw torn deckle-paper edges on background elements, faint halftone newsprint dot-texture, thin strips of aged red paper tape as physical scene/transition accents, subtle grid or graph-paper texture on card backings) — combined into one consistent handcrafted-but-photographically-real look: layered textured cardstock, corrugated cardboard, naturalistic soft-edged shadows with gentle falloff, warm tungsten key light with cool ambient fill, fine film grain, and floating dust motes.
- **Format:** 9:16 vertical, thin cinematic letterbox bars top and bottom.
- **Runtime:** 75–90 sec (15 story shots + 1 bonus CTA shot).
- **Main character:** Peter Henlein, the real early-16th-century Nuremberg locksmith and clockmaker traditionally credited with building the first small portable spring-driven timepieces (the so-called "Nuremberg eggs"), reimagined as a paper-cutout figure — a lean, slightly stooped 45-year-old craftsman, cut from warm ochre-toned cardstock with visible paper-fiber grain standing in for weathered skin, a close-cropped grey-streaked brown paper beard, short hair under a flat black wool cap cut from matte charcoal paper, a rust-brown wool doublet with a crisp white linen paper collar, a heavy dark-umber corrugated-cardboard leather apron, oversized craftsman's paper hands, and a brass-colored paper loupe hanging from a thin red paper-tape cord around his neck as his one distinguishing detail; his expression is quietly focused and intent, brows slightly drawn, mouth set in concentration.

## 5. AUDIO NOTES

- **Music arc:** Open with a sparse, intimate solo cello or nyckelharp-like string drone under Shots 1–2, evoking archival documentary stillness. From Shot 3–5, introduce a low, searching pizzicato motif that mirrors Henlein's frustration and sudden idea. Shots 6–10 (forging, coiling, cutting, assembling) build a steady mechanical rhythm section — a ticking woodblock or muted clock-like percussion layered under the strings — gradually increasing in tempo and density to mirror the mounting craftsmanship. Shot 11 drops the music to near silence but for a held suspenseful string note, creating a breath-holding gap before Shot 12's climax, where the full ensemble swells back in the instant the watch first ticks, introducing a warm, resolving melodic line. Shots 13–14 broaden the arrangement with fuller strings and a gentle brass undertone to convey triumph and spread. Shot 15 resolves the melody softly and simply, echoing the opening motif to bring the story full circle. Shot 16 lifts slightly with a brighter, more contemporary but still warm musical button to invite action.
- **Recurring SFX:** Paper rustle and flutter on every character or camera movement; soft footsteps on cobblestone for exterior shots; a low ambient tower-clock tick and distant weight-chain creak in Shots 1 and 3; forge crackle and hammer clangs in Shot 6; a fine metallic scrape for gear-cutting in Shot 8; a mechanical click-whirr for gear assembly in Shot 9; a distinct key-winding ratchet sound in Shot 11; the hero "first tick" sound — a soft, warm, singular mechanical tick — isolated and prominent in Shot 12; distant Nuremberg street ambience (murmurs, cart wheels, birdsong) layered under Shots 13–14; a gentle paper-pin "tap" sound effect in Shot 16.
- **Narrator tone:** A warm, measured documentary narrator — unhurried, intimate, slightly hushed, the tone of a museum audio guide rather than a promotional voiceover; each line delivered with quiet wonder and precise, deliberate pacing, letting small pauses breathe before key reveals (the spring, the first tick, the closing line).
